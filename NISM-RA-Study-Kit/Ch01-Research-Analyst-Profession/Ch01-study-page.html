Chapter 1 of 15

The research analyst profession

Worth 1 mark of 100Book pages 15 to 21About 15 minutes

A research analyst collects facts, makes sense of them, and tells clients whether to buy, hold or sell.

1.1

What does an analyst do?

You want a new phone. You fix a budget, shortlist brands, compare battery and camera, and pick one. That is research and analysis. An analyst does the same thing for shares and bonds, on behalf of clients.

The job has two halves.

  • Research means collecting information from many sources.
  • Analysis means processing that information to reach a decision.
Legal definitionUnder the SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations, 2014, a research analyst is a person who, for consideration (that is, for a fee), is in the business of providing research services. It includes a part-time research analyst.

Three types of analyst

Analysts are sorted by who they work for and who reads their work.

Picture three food critics. The sell-side analyst writes a newspaper review anyone can read. The buy-side analyst is the head chef's own taster, and only the kitchen hears the verdict. The independent analyst runs a paid food newsletter.

Besides these three, newspapers, media and information consolidators also publish research.

The book sums up the role in one word: the analyst is a selector. They study the company's past, judge its future, and recommend.

Buy-side reports are not in the public domain. The price target belongs to the sell-side description.
1.2

What do they study?

Analysts work top down. First the economy, then the industry, then the single company. Each layer sets the stage for the next.

Why the economy first?

Company growth depends on the economy around it. The book quotes John Maynard Keynes (1883 to 1946): governments can change economic performance by adjusting tax rates and government spending.

Why the industry next?

Each industry has different problems and different growth drivers. A bank and a cement maker live by different rules.

Why each company separately?

Rahul Dravid and Virender Sehwag were both great batsmen. One was defensive, one was aggressive. Who does better depends on the pitch and the bowlers, and even then only as a probability. Companies in the same industry also play very differently.
Market share and capacity utilisation are not money figures, but they are still quantitative. Brand power and management quality are qualitative.
1.3

How do they talk to people?

The internet gives a lot of data. It still cannot replace talking to the company directly. But there is a catch.

Why cross-check?Management may paint a rosy picture so analysts write positive reports and push the share price up. So the analyst must cross-verify management's claims before recommending.
Meeting management is like interviewing a job candidate. You read the CV first, you bring prepared questions, you call references, and you do not believe everything you hear.

The overall expectation: work with sincerity, honesty and ethics, without bias, and follow SEBI rules in letter and in spirit.

Conflict of interest is disclosed before, not after. Recording needs consent. The key contact is not always a top manager.
1.4

What makes a good one?

The job needs both number skills and judgement. A good analyst is methodical, has an enquiring mind, knows where to find relevant information, and understands business models and competition.

All exam traps in this chapter

  1. The role of an analyst is to help clients take informed decisions. Financial planning is a different job (investment adviser).
  2. The SEBI definition says for consideration and includes part-time analysts.
  3. Sell-side: public reports with a price target. Buy-side: internal only. Independent: sold by subscription.
  4. Economic data: government and RBI. Global data: IMF, ADB, World Bank.
  5. Keynes: governments change performance through tax rates and government spending.
  6. Market share and capacity utilisation are quantitative, even though they are not money figures.
  7. Always cross-verify management claims. Management may exaggerate.
  8. Ask management not to share non-public information.
  9. The most useful contact may not be from top management.
  10. Disclose conflicts beforehand. Record only with consent.

For the group discussion

  1. Pick a real company. Which of the four data levels would you check first, and why?
  2. A sell-side analyst's firm also runs that company's IPO. What could go wrong? How do the client-writing rules help?
  3. Name one qualitative and one quantitative factor for a company you know. Swap and check each other.
  4. Management tells you sales will double next year. List three people outside the company you would call to check.
