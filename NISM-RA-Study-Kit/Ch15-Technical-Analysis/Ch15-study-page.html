Technical analysis

Worth 15 marks of 100Book pages 295 to 327About 90 minutes

Read the market from its own record of price and volume: the trend, the patterns that signal a turn, and the indicators that confirm it.

15.1

What technical analysis is

Technical analysis evaluates securities from statistics of trading activity, mainly price and volume. Unlike fundamental analysis, it assumes all relevant information is already in the price. It is used across equities, currencies, commodities and crypto, especially for short to medium term trading. Chapter 4 (4.3) gave a first look.

15.2

Chart types

15.3

Dow Theory

Developed by Charles Dow through editorials between 1900 and 1902, and organised into a formal theory after his death by William Hamilton and Robert Rhea. Still a foundation of technical analysis.

Dow Theory has three trends, not four, and six tenets. The book's sample question offers "the market has four trends" as the false tenet.

15.4 Understanding market trends

The book's exampleNifty 50, 2020 to 2021: a bullish primary trend after the COVID crash; a secondary correction of about 7% in September to October 2020 on global risk-off sentiment; then new highs by early 2021.
The primary trend is the tide, the secondary trend is the waves, and the tertiary trend is the ripples. A ripple against the tide does not turn it.

Tertiary trends carry risks: noise in choppy markets, whipsaws (false breakouts), and overtrading, which raises costs and risk.

15.5

Reversal patterns (candlesticks)

Each candle shows open, high, low and close. The real body is the gap between open and close; the thin lines above and below are the shadows (wicks). Below, green candles closed higher and red candles closed lower. The highlighted candles form the pattern.

Count the candles. One: hanging man, hammer. Two: bullish and bearish engulfing, dark cloud cover, piercing. Three: morning star, evening star. Top or bottom: dark cloud cover forms near the top of a congestion area; piercing near the bottom.
15.6

Consolidation patterns

Pauses within a trend, usually followed by a breakout. Dashed lines are the trendlines drawn along the highs and lows.

15.7

Support and resistance

  • Breakout: price moves decisively beyond the level with volume. False breakout: a brief breach that quickly reverses, trapping traders. Volume and candlestick confirmation help tell them apart.
  • Strength of a level: more touches, higher volume at the level, more time spent near it, and more recent levels all make it stronger.
  • Uses: bounce trades (buy near support, sell near resistance), breakout trades, pullback entries on a retest, and stop-losses just below support or above resistance.
  • Limits: levels are zones, not exact prices; drawing them is subjective; in strong trends they are respected less; news can override them.
  • Advanced ideas: order blocks, liquidity pools (where stop orders cluster), VWAP and anchored VWAP.

Book's case notes: Nifty found strong support around 24,350 from May to September 2025; Reliance faced resistance around 1,300 from October 2021 to July 2023 before breaking out in January 2024.

15.8 Trendlines and channels

Validation

Rising volume on breakouts; candlesticks (engulfing, hammer, shooting star) at the lines; RSI divergence or MACD crossovers near boundaries; lines visible on higher timeframes carry more weight.

Common pitfalls

Forcing lines to fit a bias; ignoring breakouts and not redrawing after invalidation; relying on lines without other confirmation.

15.9

Technical indicators

15.9.1 Moving averages

A smoothed line of average closing prices (daily, weekly or monthly). Rising MA: uptrend; falling: downtrend. MAs are lagging indicators but good for major trends. When monthly, weekly and daily MAs point the same way, the trend is likely to continue. Trading at or near a rising MA can be a good time to buy.

15.9.2 MACD

Default MACD line = difference between the 26 EMA and the 12 EMA. Slow line = 9 EMA of the default line.
  • Shows shifts in momentum and can warn early of a trend change. At times it leads price.
  • Buy when both lines are rising and the fast line crosses above the slow line; also when the fast line, already above, turns up again after nearly touching the slow line.
  • Divergence: price makes a higher high but MACD a lower high: losing upward momentum. Price makes a new low but MACD a higher low: losing downward momentum, likely to bottom out.
  • The zero line separates confirmed bull and bear markets, but a crossover of zero should not be used for trading.
  • Steep MACD: a powerful move; shallow: lack of power. A bulge of the fast line far above the slow line: a buying climax, possibly followed by a sideways market or mild correction.
  • MACD can go flat or turn without a trend reversal; it shows momentum, not price, so use it with other indicators and price action.
  • A double top or bottom in MACD suggests a likely reversal. Avoid stocks whose weekly or monthly MACD is clearly trending down.

15.9.4 ADX (Average Directional Index)

  • Measures the strength of a trend, not its direction. Values from 0 to 100; default setting 14 bars.
  • Below 25: weak or no trend. A rising ADX, especially above 25: a strong trend.
  • Plotted with +DMI and −DMI. Buy when +DMI crosses above −DMI and ADX is rising; sell when +DMI crosses below −DMI and ADX is rising. Ignore crossovers when ADX is weak.

15.9.5 RSC (Relative Strength Comparative)

  • Also called the Relative Strength Indicator or Price Relative Indicator. A ratio chart comparing one security with another, such as a stock against the Nifty 50 or its sector.
  • Use about a 5 to 6 year period. Normalised to 100: above 100 means outperforming the benchmark; below 100, underperforming.
  • A good buy: RSC below 100 for a long time, then turning up and crossing 100.

15.9.6 OBV (On Balance Volume)

  • Add the day's volume to a running total if the close is higher than yesterday's; subtract it if lower.
  • OBV should rise with prices and fall with them. In a divergence, volume often leads price, warning of a reversal.
  • Read OBV 1 (the line) with OBV 20 (its 20-period average). Buy when OBV 1 crosses above a rising OBV 20; sell when it crosses below a falling OBV 20.
  • A pronounced weakening of OBV on long-term charts can mean smart money is quietly exiting. A higher high in price without a higher OBV: buying pressure is fading.
  • The book's example: Maharashtra Scooters' weekly OBV turned up from May 2023, and the stock delivered about 4 times from there.
The book's closing viewTechnical analysis is a guide, not a guarantee. It works best with discipline, risk management, an understanding of market context, and combined with macroeconomic and sentiment analysis. Critics call it self-fulfilling, yet its wide use keeps it relevant.

All exam traps in this chapter

  1. Technical analysis uses price and volume and assumes price discounts everything.
  2. Point and figure ignores time and volume. Renko uses fixed price bricks. Heikin-Ashi averages prices.
  3. Line charts use closing prices only; bar charts show OHLC.
  4. Dow Theory: Charles Dow, editorials 1900 to 1902; formalised by Hamilton and Rhea; six tenets; three trends.
  5. Primary trend phases: accumulation, public participation, distribution.
  6. Indices must confirm each other; volume should rise in the primary trend's direction.
  7. Secondary trends last about 3 weeks to 3 months and retrace 1/3 to 2/3 of the primary move.
  8. Tertiary trends: usually under 3 weeks.
  9. Hanging man: bearish, after an up move, needs a lower close next. Hammer: bullish, after a decline. Shadow at least twice the body.
  10. Bullish engulfing: stronger after four or more red candles. Only real bodies count.
  11. Dark cloud cover: top of congestion; red closes below the green's midpoint. Piercing: bottom; green covers at least half the red.
  12. Morning star bullish, evening star bearish: three candles each.
  13. Ascending triangle: flat top, higher lows, bullish continuation. Descending: flat bottom, lower highs, bearish continuation.
  14. Triangle target: height at its thickest point, from the breakout point.
  15. Flag: small rectangle after a flagpole; pennant: small triangle.
  16. Support is demand (a floor); resistance is supply (a ceiling). Broken support becomes resistance.
  17. A trendline needs at least two points; three or more make it more reliable.
  18. Moving averages are lagging. 13, 21, 34 EMA; price supported near the 34 EMA in a bull market.
  19. MACD line = 26 EMA minus 12 EMA; slow line = 9 EMA of it. Do not trade zero-line crossovers.
  20. RSI above 70 overbought, below 30 oversold. Stock new low, RSI not: bullish divergence.
  21. RSI rarely below 44 to 45 in a bull market, rarely above 50 to 55 in a bear market.
  22. ADX measures strength, not direction; below 25 weak; default 14.
  23. RSC above 100: outperforming the benchmark.
  24. OBV adds volume on up days and subtracts on down days; read with its 20-period average.
