The securities market

Worth 2 marks of 100Book pages 22 to 54About 50 minutes

A securities market moves savers' money to businesses that need it, and lets savers get out whenever they want.

2.1

What is a security?

A security is a financial claim you can transfer to someone else. It is one of two things:

  • a promise to pay you back (debt, like a bond), or
  • a share of ownership in a company (equity).

Companies, financial institutions and the government issue them. Investors buy them. The investor gets a return. The issuer gets money, at a cost. The words security, instrument and product mean the same thing in this book.

The big idea: long term becomes short term

A 10-year bond is like a 10-hour train journey. The company needs the train to run the full 10 hours. You may want to get off after 2. The secondary market is the station where you step off and a new passenger takes your seat. The train never stops, and the company is not affected.

This ability to buy and sell easily, close to the market price, is called liquidity.

Borrowers are the sellers of securities. Investors are the buyers. It feels backwards, so it gets tested.

The legal definition

"Securities" is defined in Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA).

2.2

The products

Every product has a different mix of risk and return. The book gives each one an "ID card": who issues it, who invests, how it is sold, and who regulates it. The regulator is the part most likely to be asked.

Debentures, bonds and notes

All three are loans. The names overlap, and the book says you cannot draw hard lines. Rough usage:

  • Bonds: umbrella term in the US. Often government, often longer than 10 years. In the UK, government bonds are called gilts.
  • Debentures: more common in the UK usage and for companies.
  • Notes: shorter and medium term.

Debentures can be secured (backed by collateral) or unsecured.

Short-term debt (up to one year) has three instruments:

Domestic, foreign, euro and masala bonds

Four questions decide the name: Who issues? In which country? In what currency? What is that country's own currency?

Who carries the currency risk?Borrowing in dollars is cheaper, because rates are lower. But if the dollar rises against the rupee, the Indian issuer must find more rupees to repay. So with foreign currency bonds, the issuer carries the risk. With masala bonds, the foreign investor carries it, because repayment is in rupees.
"Euro bond" has nothing to do with Europe or the euro currency. It only means the currency is different from the local currency where it is sold.

Two dates to remember: the first masala bond was issued in November 2014 by the International Finance Corporation and listed on the London Stock Exchange. The book's foreign bond example: Delhi International Airport (a GMR company) issued USD bonds in the US in February 2020.

Convertible debentures

A loan that may turn into shares. Terms are fixed at the time of issue.

Preference shares

Called "shares", but they behave half like a loan.

Depository receipts (DRs)

A DR lets people in one country trade shares of a company from another country, in their own currency, on their own exchange.

A cloakroom token. You hand your coat to the cloakroom and get a token. The token stands for the coat. You can hand the token to someone else, who can later claim the coat.
SEBI's IDR rules: a limit on money raised in India, a one-year lock-in on converting IDRs into shares, and IDRs are available only to resident Indians.

More hybrids, one line each

Pooled products

Indices

An index tracks the market using a sample of shares. Most big indices are market-cap weighted: bigger companies count for more. Shares are picked for liquidity, free float and market cap, and the list is reviewed from time to time.

Commodities

A commodity is a basic good where every unit is the same, so units can be swapped.

A 10-gram gold bar is a commodity. You do not care which bar you get. A gold necklace is not, because you might like one design over another even at the same weight.

How the market is built

Two parts that need each other:

Ways to issue securities

OFS is "a secondary market transaction done through the primary market route". Money goes to the sellers, not the company. And "FPO" can mean Follow-on Public Offer or Farmer Producer Organisation. Read the context.

The secondary market

Two ways to trade:

Novation: the key ideaThe clearing corporation becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. You never depend on the stranger on the other side.
Like an online marketplace that holds the money. You do not need to trust the seller. You trust the platform, and it deals with the seller.

Guaranteeing every trade is risky for the clearing corporation, so it collects margins:

  • Initial (upfront) margin: a percentage of trade value, based on Value at Risk.
  • Peak margin.
  • Mark-to-market (MTM) margin: the paper loss on an open trade due to price moves.
2.4

Who is in the market?

A long list. Sort it by what job each player does, and it is easy to hold.

Depository and DP are like a bank and its branch. The depository is head office (NSDL or CDSL). The DP is the branch where you open your account.
Sub-brokers were abolished. SEBI decided on 21 June 2018, effective 1 April 2019. They had to become authorised persons or trading members.

Who can be a DP: public financial institutions, scheduled commercial banks, RBI-approved foreign banks, state financial corporations, custodians, stockbrokers, clearing corporations, NBFCs, and registrars and share transfer agents.

2.5

Kinds of deals

When does settlement happen?

T is the trade date. The number after it is how many days later money and goods change hands.

The four derivatives

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else: a share, an index, wheat, an interest rate.

Swap in one lineThe borrower pays a fixed rate to a swap dealer and receives a floating rate (T-bill plus spread). The floating money received pays the floating loan. What is left is a fixed payment. Only interest is exchanged; the principal (the notional) is not.

Four motives

Pledging shares

Taking a loan against your shares.

  • Pledgor = the investor who borrows. Pledgee = the lender.
  • Pledged shares stay in the pledgor's demat account, but are blocked.
  • Once the loan is repaid, they are unpledged.
  • On default, the pledgee can sell them, after giving notice and a fair chance to repay.
Like leaving your gold at a jeweller for a loan, except here the gold stays in your own locker with a lock the lender controls.
2.6

Paper to electronic

All exam traps in this chapter

  1. Borrowers sell securities; investors buy them.
  2. Securities are defined in Section 2(h) of SCRA, 1956. ULIPs are excluded. Electronic Gold Receipts were added on 24 December 2021.
  3. Debt regulators include RBI; equity regulators do not. Warrants, mutual funds and ETFs: SEBI.
  4. T-bills: government. Commercial paper: highly rated companies. CDs: banks.
  5. Foreign bond: local currency of the country where sold. Euro bond: a different currency. Masala bond: rupees, sold abroad.
  6. Foreign currency bond: issuer carries currency risk. Masala bond: investor carries it.
  7. First masala bond: IFC, November 2014, London Stock Exchange.
  8. OCD: holder chooses to convert. FCCBs are usually optionally convertible and regulated by RBI under FEMA.
  9. Preference shares: no voting rights. Cumulative carries unpaid dividend forward.
  10. Sponsored DRs can list; unsponsored trade OTC. GDRs trade in more than one country. IDR: one-year lock-in, resident Indians only.
  11. Sensex 30 (BSE), Nifty 50 (NSE), SX40 40 (MSEI). Most indices are market-cap weighted.
  12. Close-ended fund units must be listed. ETFs have lower expense ratios.
  13. REIT: 80% in real estate. InvIT: 90% in infra. Both distribute at least 90%.
  14. IPO: retail at least 35% (up to Rs 2 lakh), QIB at most 50%. Anchor: Rs 10 crore plus, up to 60% of QIB portion, one day early.
  15. OFS: no new shares; money to the sellers. Rights: exercise, transfer or lapse. Bonus: free, from retained earnings.
  16. Private placement: up to 50 investors as per the book. QIP is for listed companies only.
  17. Sweat equity: Section 54, Companies Act, 2013.
  18. Novation: clearing corporation is buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. Initial margin is based on Value at Risk.
  19. Two depositories: NSDL and CDSL. Sub-brokers ended 1 April 2019.
  20. P-Notes are issued by FPIs to overseas investors who are not registered with SEBI.
  21. AIF Cat I: VC and angel funds. Cat II: PE and real estate funds. Cat III: hedge funds and PIPE funds.
  22. Cash T+0, Tom T+1, Spot T+2.
  23. Option buyer pays premium and has a right. Writer receives premium and has an obligation.
  24. Hedging aims for no gain, no loss. Arbitrage is simultaneous buy and sell.
  25. Pledged shares stay in the pledgor's account, blocked.
  26. Remat gives back distinctive numbers.
